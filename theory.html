      Quick reference

      The twelve notes, in order. Every step is one fret.

      01 — Basics

      The building blocks

      Understand the building blocks of music before applying them to the guitar.

      8 lessons

      1What is sound?

      A sound is something moving back and forth in the air. Pluck a string and it swings from side to side, pushing the air around it into waves that reach your ear. Nothing travels down the string to you — the string just shakes, and the air carries the shaking.

      Frequency is how many times per second it swings, measured in hertz (Hz). Amplitude is how far it swings. Frequency is what you hear as pitch: faster shaking sounds higher. Amplitude is what you hear as loudness — a harder pluck is louder, not higher.

      2The musical alphabet

      Note names use seven letters and then stop: A B C D E F G. After G the sequence starts again at A, and that A is one octave above the one you began on. Seven letters, repeating for as long as the instrument has range.

      Those seven are the natural notes. They are not evenly spaced: between most of them there is room for one more pitch, and those in-between pitches are named with a sharp or a flat rather than with a letter of their own. That is how seven letters end up covering twelve pitches.

      3The twelve pitch classes

      The chromatic system divides the octave into 12 pitch classes: twelve named pitches, after which the names begin again one octave higher. A pitch class is the name without the octave — every C anywhere on the instrument belongs to pitch class C. On a guitar each step from one pitch class to the next is exactly one fret.

      Enharmonic equivalents are two names for one pitch: C♯ and D♭ are the same fret, played the same way, and which name is used depends on the key the music is written in. This site writes sharps, so you will see C♯ rather than D♭.

      4Pitch

      Pitch is how high or low a note sounds, and it comes straight from frequency: higher frequency, higher pitch. To name pitches everyone has to agree on one reference, and the common one is A4 = 440 Hz — the A above middle C. Every other note is worked out from it.

      Other references exist — some orchestras tune to 442 or 443 Hz, and older music is often played at 415 — but 440 is what a guitar tuner means unless it says otherwise. The tuner on this site measures against 440.

      5Octaves

      An octave is the same note higher or lower. Play an A, then play the next A up: it sounds like the same note, just further up — which is why both are called A.

      The reason is arithmetic. Going up an octave doubles the frequency; going down halves it. If A4 is 440 Hz then A5 is 880 Hz and A3 is 220 Hz. That doubling is what the ear recognises as "the same note", and it is written as a ratio of 2:1.

      6Naming the octaves

      The number after a note name says which octave it is in. A4 is the A at 440 Hz; A3 is the A below it, A5 the A above. Same letter, different floor of the building.

      The floors are counted from the bottom of hearing upwards, and every octave also has an older name — the one a piano teacher is more likely to use. Both name the same eight floors.

      One trap: the number changes at C, not at A. So B3 and C4 are neighbours — one fret apart — even though the number jumps between them. The open B string is B3 and the open thin E string is E4.

      7Sharps and flats

      A sharp (♯) raises a note by one semitone — one fret up. A flat (♭) lowers it by one semitone — one fret down. So C♯ is one fret above C, and D♭ is one fret below D. Those are the same fret, which is why the note has two names.

      But two pairs of letters are already only one semitone apart, with nothing between them: E to F, and B to C. There is no E♯ fret to find, because F is already there. This single fact is what makes the fretboard confusing until you know it — and obvious afterwards.

      8Semitones and whole tones

      A semitone is the smallest step in this system: one fret. A whole tone is two semitones: two frets. Scales and chords are built by counting these steps, so it is worth being able to do it without thinking.

      Walk up the low E string and you can see it. E to F is a semitone — one fret, because of the rule above. F to G is a whole tone — two frets, with F♯ in between.

      02 — The Guitar Fretboard

      Notes on the neck

      Learn how musical notes are mapped onto the guitar neck.

      E · A · D · G · B · E

      1Standard tuning

      Strings are numbered from the thinnest down: string 1 is the thin E at the bottom of the neck as you look at it, string 6 is the thick E on top. In standard tuning, from thickest to thinnest, they are E A D G B E.

      The two E strings are two octaves apart — same letter, same name, very different pitch. Everywhere on this site the neck is drawn with string 6 at the bottom and string 1 at the top, the way it looks when the guitar is in your hands.

      Those six notes are not a random pick. Tuning starts from E2 on the thickest string, and every string after it is a perfect fourth — five frets — above the one before. Every string but one: from string 3 to string 2, G to B, the step is a major third, four frets.

      That one odd step is worth remembering, because almost every oddity of the guitar comes from it: it is why the same chord shape changes on the top two strings, and why the octave shapes further on shift by a fret once they cross the B string. In exchange, chords stay inside a four-fret reach instead of demanding a sixth finger.

      2Strings and frets

      Each fret shortens the string a little, which makes it shake faster. One fret up is one semitone up. Twelve frets up is twelve semitones — a whole octave, so the note repeats and the frequency has doubled.

      The board below is the whole neck with every note named. Press any fret to hear it and read it off; the panel underneath tells you the note, where it is, and how fast it shakes.

      3Why E–F and B–C are different

      Most neighbouring letters are a whole tone apart: C to D, D to E, F to G, G to A, A to B. Two frets, with a sharp in the middle.

      Two are not. E to F is one fret, and B to C is one fret. That is why the first fret of the low E string is F rather than E♯, and why the first fret of the B string is C. Once you expect those two, naming notes along a string stops being guesswork.

      4Finding notes on the neck

      There is a method, and it is duller than it is difficult. Learn the six open strings — E A D G B E. Then walk up chromatically from whichever one you need, remembering that E–F and B–C have no fret between them.

      On the A string that gives: open A, 1 = A♯, 2 = B, 3 = C (no gap after B), 4 = C♯, 5 = D. Use the string chooser in the board above to read one string at a time — that is how it actually sticks.

      5Fret markers

      The dots inlaid in the neck are there so your hand knows where it is without looking twice. They sit at frets 3, 5, 7, 9, then a double marker at 12, then 15, 17, 19, 21 and a double at 24 — the pattern repeats after the twelfth.

      The double marker at 12 is the important one: the twelfth fret is the octave. Every string played there is the open string again, one octave higher, and the second half of the neck repeats the first.

      The boards on this site draw 15 frets, so the markers you will see on them are 3, 5, 7 and 9, the double one at 12, and 15.

      6The twelfth fret

      Twelve frets is twelve semitones, and twelve semitones is an octave. So the twelfth fret of any string is that string's open note again, one octave higher, at exactly double the frequency.

      That is what the double marker is telling you, and it is why the neck above the twelfth fret repeats the neck below it: fret 13 is fret 1 an octave up, fret 14 is fret 2, and so on to the end of the board.

      7Find every note

      Pick a note and every place it sits on the neck lights up at once. One pitch class turns up several times across six strings, and seeing all of them together is what turns the neck from a grid into a map.

      Two patterns are worth looking for: on any one string the note comes back every twelve frets, and the copies on neighbouring strings always sit in the same shapes — which is what stage 04 is about.

      03 — Intervals

      The distance between notes

      Intervals are the distance between two notes. They are the foundation of scales, chords and melodies.

      Perfect 5th

      1What is an interval?

      An interval is the distance between two pitches — nothing more. Because one fret is one semitone, on a guitar that musical distance is also a physical one you can see and count.

      Play the open A string and the note four frets up on the same string. That distance is four semitones, which is a major third, and it will be a major third wherever on the neck you play it. Intervals are the reason a shape you learn in one place works in every other.

      2Intervals from unison to octave

      Every chromatic distance from unison up to the octave has a common name, a short code you will see on charts, and a fixed number of semitones. Press any row to hear it played from the open A string.

      One row carries two names. Six semitones is the tritone, and depending on how the two notes are spelled it is called an augmented 4th or a diminished 5th — both mean the same six semitones. There is a lesson on it further down.

      3Semitone distance

      Written as a number of semitones, the whole set becomes a ruler you can count along the neck: 1 is a minor 2nd, 2 a major 2nd, 3 a minor 3rd, 4 a major 3rd, and so on to 12, the octave.

      4Perfect, major and minor

      An interval is not named by its semitone count alone. The number in the name — 2nd, 3rd, 4th — counts letter names from one note to the other, and the word in front of it says how wide that distance actually is.

      There are two families. The perfect intervals are the unison, the 4th, the 5th and the octave: each comes in one basic size. The major and minor intervals are the 2nd, 3rd, 6th and 7th: each comes in two sizes a semitone apart, and the minor one is the narrower.

      5Augmented and diminished

      Two more quality words, and both work by moving a known interval one semitone. Augmented is one semitone wider than the perfect or major interval of the same number. Diminished is one semitone narrower than the perfect or minor one.

      The resulting distance usually has a plainer name as well, and both names are right — they describe the same fret from different directions. C to F♯ is an augmented 4th because F is the fourth letter up from C; C to G♭ is a diminished 5th because G is the fifth. The fret is the same one.

      6The tritone

      Six semitones: exactly half an octave, and the only interval that inverts into itself. The name comes from the three whole tones it is made of.

      It has two spellings and both are correct. Up from C to F♯ it is an augmented 4th; up from C to G♭ it is a diminished 5th. Six semitones either way, the same fret on a guitar — which name is used follows the key and the chord it sits in.

      On its own it sounds unresolved, which is why it is worth knowing by ear. Inside a dominant seventh chord, where the 3rd and the flattened 7th are a tritone apart, it is completely ordinary — and it is what makes that chord want to move somewhere.

      8Interval inversion

      Invert an interval and the two notes swap places: the lower one moves up an octave, or the upper one moves down one. C up to E is a major 3rd; E up to C is a minor 6th. The same two note names, a different distance.

      An interval and its inversion always add up to twelve semitones — one octave. A major 3rd is 4, so its inversion is 8, which is a minor 6th. Perfect stays perfect, major becomes minor and minor becomes major, and the tritone inverts into itself.

      9How they sound

      Intervals have a character, and it is worth having words for it. Treat what follows as a first impression, not a rule: context changes everything. A tritone is harsh on its own and completely ordinary inside a dominant seventh chord.

      04 — Fretboard Shapes

      The shapes that repeat

      Why an interval between two strings is always the same shape, and how to work any of them out.

      Octave

      1One string is five frets

      Every string is tuned a perfect fourth above the one below it, and a perfect fourth is five semitones. So stepping across to the next string is the same move as sliding five frets up the string you are on. That single sentence is where every shape on this page comes from.

      With one exception, the one from stage 02: from string 3 to string 2 the step is a major third, so that crossing is worth four frets, not five. Every shape that crosses the B string shifts by a fret because of it.

      That is why the list below holds two octave shapes rather than one. They are the same octave: the second is the first, moved a fret along to pay for the B string. The same happens to every other shape that reaches across it.

      2Where a shape comes from

      Now any shape is just addition. Count what the string crossings are worth, add the frets you slide, and the total is the distance in semitones — which is the interval, by name.

      The shape you pick is drawn straight onto the neck: an arrow for every string crossed, carrying what that crossing costs, and an arrow for the fret slide.

      It works the other way round too: press any note on the neck, then a second one, and the distance between them is counted out the same way. If that pair is one of the shapes in the list, it lights up. A third press starts again.
